What We Do

We seek to shift NLP beyond the dominant heuristic practice by grounding it in rigorous theoretical and interdisciplinary discourse. Our aim is to foster dialogue shaped by diverse fields, encouraging critical reflection, shared learning, and the development of more socially grounded perspectives and methods in NLP.

To this end, instead of functioning only as a paper reading group, we host a range of focused activities on defending substantive claims, interrogating taken-for-granted practices, and exploring conceptual resources from beyond NLP. Through these sessions, we hope to expand our epistemic boundaries and develop more theoretically grounded ways of doing NLP.

Modes of Engagement

Currently, we are exploring three modes of engagement, each anchored in one or a few papers per session:

Mode 1

Bring a Positive Claim

Participants come prepared with a claim drawn from NLP research or practice that they find meaningful and are willing to defend, along with, optionally, a set of known critiques they have chosen to engage rather than avoid. The group's role is to interrogate the claim, assumptions, and boundaries of its validity.

See examples
  • Reasoning benchmarks have diagnostic value even if they fail as measures of LLMs' general reasoning ability.
  • Chain-of-thought reasoning improves AI safety classification performance.
  • Integrating labels or preferences from more than one annotator makes models generate more diverse responses.
  • Fine-tuning with culturally diverse datasets improves models' cultural coverage.
Mode 2

Practices on Trial

Participants identify a method, metric, or framing in NLP that tends to go unquestioned and is treated as standard, neutral, or simply the way things are done. The discussion moves beyond asking whether the practice works, and instead examines it as a social and epistemic phenomenon: why it emerged, what assumptions it encodes, whose interests it serves, and what it makes harder to see. The goal is interpretive rather than evaluative.

See examples
  • Why do math, coding, and deductive logic tasks stand in for "reasoning"? Are they sufficient?
  • How did step-by-step decomposition (chain-of-thought) become synonymous with reasoning, and what kinds of reasoning does it sideline?
  • Why are annotators treated as interchangeable labor rather than socially situated knowers?
  • How did minimally paid crowdwork become "cost-effective" standard infrastructure?
  • Why is "English first, then multilingual" the default, and what gets baked in that can't be fixed later?
Mode 3

Epistemic Shift

Participants bring a concept, method, or framework from outside NLP and make the case for its relevance. The discussion unpacks what the idea actually means in its home discipline, traces where it may already be quietly shaping NLP, and explores what it would look like to apply it more deliberately. The goal is to import dimensions from other disciplines that could reorient how NLP approaches core problems, expose blind spots, reframe questions, or suggest novel methodologies.

See examples
  • Informal, pragmatic, and rhetorical reasoning from informal logic
  • Situated cognition from cognitive science
  • Standpoint epistemology from feminist theory
  • Pluriversality from decolonial theory
